The extension is the inclusion of the hoop terms. These elements are thus one-dimensional, deforming in a radial plane. The Cartesian coordinates in this plane are r (radius) and z (axial position). Distance along the shell reference surface in such a plane is measured by the material coordinate S (see [Figure 1](simathe-c-axishells.md#simathe-c-axishells__simathe-c-stmaxishellfig)). Figure 1. Axisymmetric shell.    Interpolation and integration The 2-node element (SAX1) uses one-point integration of the linear interpolation function for the distribution of loads. The mass matrix is lumped. The 3-node element (SAX2) uses two-point integration of a quadratic interpolation function for the stiffness and three-point integration of a quadratic interpolation function for the distribution of loads. SAX2 uses a consistent mass matrix. All integrations use the Gauss method. The integration through the thickness follows the usual numerical or exact scheme used in Abaqus.  Theory This shell theory allows for finite strains and rotations of the shell. The strain measure used is chosen to give a close approximation (accurate to second-order terms) to log strain. Thus, the theory is intended for direct application to cases involving inelastic or hypoelastic deformation where the stress-strain behavior is given in terms of Kirchhoff stress (“true” stress in the usual engineering literature) and log strain, such as metal plasticity. The theory is approximate, but the approximations are not rigorously justified: they are introduced for simplicity and seem reasonable. These approximations are as follows:   1. A “thinness” assumption is made. This means that, at all times, only    terms up to first order with respect to the thickness direction coordinate are    included. 2. The thinning of the shell caused by stretching parallel to its reference    surface is assumed to be uniform through the thickness and defined by an    incompressibility condition on the reference surface of the shell. Obviously    this is a relatively coarse approximation, especially in the case where a shell    is subjected to pure bending. It is adopted because it is simple and models the    effect of thinning associated with membrane straining: this is considered to be    of primary importance in the type of applications envisioned, such as the    failure of pipes and vessels subjected to over-pressurization. 3. The thinning of the shell is assumed to occur smoothly—that is to say,    gradients of the thinning with respect to position on the reference surface are    assumed to be negligible. This means that localization effects, such as necking    of the shell, are only modeled in a very coarse way. Again, the reason for    adopting this approximation is simplicity—details of localization effects are    not important to the type of application for which the elements are designed. 4. All stresses except those parallel to the reference surface are    neglected; and, for the nonnegligible stresses, plane stress theory is assumed.    As with (c) above, this precludes detailed localization studies, but introduces    considerable simplification into the formulation. 5. Plane sections remain plane. This has been shown to be consistent with    the thinness assumption, (a) above, for most material models. Here it is simply    assumed without further justification. 6. Transverse shears are assumed to be small, and the material response to    such deformation is assumed to be linear elastic. Transverse shear is    introduced because the elements used are of the “reduced integration, penalty”    type (see    [Hughes    et al., 1977](simathe-c-references.md#simathe-c-references__simathe-c-ref-hughes-taylor), for example). In these elements position on the reference    surface and rotation of lines initially orthogonal to the reference surface are    interpolated independently: the transverse shear stiffness is then viewed as a    penalty term imposing the necessary constraint at selected (reduced    integration) points. This transverse shear stiffness is the actual elastic    value for relatively thick shells. For thinner cases the penalty must be    reduced for numerical reasons—this is done in    Abaqus    in the manner described in    [Hughes    et al. (1977)](simathe-c-references.md#simathe-c-references__simathe-c-ref-hughes-taylor).   The theory is now described in detail. The concepts are taken from various sources, most especially [Budiansky and Sanders (1963)](simathe-c-references.md#simathe-c-references__simathe-c-ref-budiansky) and [Rodal and Witmer (1979)](simathe-c-references.md#simathe-c-references__simathe-c-ref-rodal). The position of a material point in the shell is given by  (1)x1=x+λt⁢η⁢n,  where  x⁢(θ1,θ2)  is the position of a point on the reference surface of the shell;  n⁢(θ1,θ2)  is a unit vector in the “thickness” direction, this direction being initially orthogonal to the reference surface;  λt  is the stretch of the shell in the thickness direction;  η  measures position with respect to the thickness direction, in the reference configuration; and  (θ1,    θ2)  are material coordinates in the reference surface.  The assumptions listed above imply that λt=λt⁢(θ1,    θ2) only and that ∂λt/∂θ1,∂λt/∂θ2 are small quantities. [Equation 1](simathe-c-axishells.md#simathe-c-axishells-t-Theory-sma-topic2__simathe-c-axishells-eq1) is written at the end of an increment, and at the start of an increment the same equation is written as  (2)X1=X+λto⁢η⁢N.  The metric at the end of an increment is  (3)∂⁡x1∂⁡θα⋅∂⁡x1∂⁡θβ=(∂⁡x∂⁡θα+λt⁢η⁢∂⁡n∂⁡θα)⋅(∂⁡x∂⁡θβ+λt⁢n⁢∂⁡n∂⁡θβ)≈∂⁡x∂⁡θα⋅∂⁡x∂⁡θβ+η⁢λt⁢(∂⁡x∂⁡θα⋅∂⁡n∂⁡θβ+∂⁡n∂⁡θα⋅∂⁡x∂⁡θβ)=gα⁢β+η⁢λt⁢bα⁢β,        say,  where  gα⁢β=∂⁡x∂⁡θα⋅∂⁡x∂⁡θβ⁢ is the metric of the reference surface,  and  bα⁢β=∂⁡x∂⁡θα⋅∂⁡n∂⁡θβ+∂⁡n∂⁡θα⋅∂⁡x∂⁡θβ  is an approximation to the curvature tensor (second fundamental form) of the reference surface. bα⁢β would be precisely the curvature tensor as it is usually defined if  n⋅∂⁡x∂⁡θα=0.  This is only approximately true for these elements, because a small transverse shear is allowed.  At the start of the increment the same quantities are  (4)∂⁡X1∂⁡θα⋅∂⁡X1∂⁡θβ=Gα⁢β+η⁢λto⁢Bα⁢β.  Axisymmetric shells undergoing axisymmetric deformations have the great simplification that principal directions do not rotate. Thus, by assuming that θ1 and θ2 are oriented in these principal directions (θ1 is meridional and θ2 is circumferential), the stretch ratios that occur within the increment in these directions are written as  Δλα1=(∂⁡x1∂⁡θα⋅∂⁡x1∂⁡θα    /    ∂⁡X1∂⁡θα⋅∂⁡X1∂⁡θα)-12,  where from this point onward the summation convention has been dropped for indexes α and β. Using [Equation 3](simathe-c-axishells.md#simathe-c-axishells-t-Theory-sma-topic2__simathe-c-axishells-eq3) and [Equation 4](simathe-c-axishells.md#simathe-c-axishells-t-Theory-sma-topic2__simathe-c-axishells-eq4) and truncating to first order in η then gives  (5)Δ⁢λα1≈Δ⁢λα⁢(1+η⁢λt⁢Δ⁢kα⁢α),  where  Δ⁢λα=(gα⁢α/Gα⁢α)12  and  Δ⁢kα⁢α=λt⁢bα⁢αgα⁢α-λtoΔt⁢Bα⁢αGα⁢α.  The incremental strain, Δ⁢εα⁢α1 , is defined as  Δ⁢εα⁢α1=2⁢(Δ⁢λα1-1Δ⁢λα1+1).  Because this expression approximates the increment of log strain correctly to second-order terms, it can be thought of as a central difference approximation for the rate of deformation. This expression is used because we anticipate that strain increments of a maximum of 20 percent per increment will be used: at that magnitude the difference between this definition of incremental strain and the increment of log strain is about 1%, which seems to be acceptable (4 % of the increment). At lower—and probably more typical—values of strain increment, the error is very much less. Again expanding to first order in the thickness direction coordinate, η, we obtain  Δ⁢εα⁢α1≈Δ⁢εα⁢α+η⁢4⁢Δ⁢λα(1+Δ⁢λα)2⁢Δ⁢kα⁢α,  where Δ⁢εα⁢α is the incremental strain of the reference surface—the membrane strain. Now consider the term  4⁢Δ⁢λα(1+Δ⁢λα)2.  Write Δ⁢λα=1+e, where e represents the change in length per unit length that occurs within the increment (the “nominal strain” with respect to the configuration at the beginning of the increment).  Then  4⁢Δ⁢λα(1+Δ⁢λα)2=1+e(1+e/2)2=(1+e)⁢(1-e+34⁢e2-12⁢e3+⋯)=(1-14⁢e2+14⁢e3-⋯).  Again, if e≈​ 20 percent, this means that  4⁢Δ⁢λα(1+Δ⁢λα)2≈1-.01+0⁢(e3),  and so once again using the argument that practical applications will involve strain increments of no more than a few percent, we approximate  4⁢Δ⁢λα(1+Δ⁢λα)2≈1.  This then gives  (6)Δ⁢εα⁢α1≈Δ⁢εα⁢α+η⁢λt⁢Δ⁢kα⁢α.  The stretch ratio in the thickness direction is assumed to be defined by the following relation on the reference surface:  λt=(λ1⁢λ2)-ν/1-ν⁢λtth,  where λtth is the thickness stretch ratio caused by thermal expansion.  From the definition of Δ⁢kα⁢α,  (7)Δ⁢kα⁢α=bα⁢αgα⁢α-1λt⁢Bα⁢αGα⁢α.  The transverse shear strains are written as  (8)Δ⁢γα=∂⁡x∂⁡θα⋅n-∂⁡X∂⁡θα⋅N.  This simple form is used because these strains are always assumed to be small. This completes the statement of the incremental strain definitions, and so—together with a virtual work statement to represent equilibrium—a theory is available. However, it is necessary to satisfy the minimum requirement that the theory provide constant strain under appropriate motions. This is essential if the theory is to be suitable for many practical cases, most especially those involving thermal loading. Interestingly, the theory in [Rodal and Witmer (1979)](simathe-c-references.md#simathe-c-references__simathe-c-ref-rodal) appears to violate this requirement. To achieve this, a modified incremental curvature change measure is defined as  Δ⁢k~α⁢β=Δ⁢kα⁢β+sym⁢(ραγ⁢Δ⁢εγ⁢β),  where  ραβ⁢ is a tensor, defined as follows.  We know that the radii of curvature of the α-line at the end and at the beginning of an increment are given by  1rα=bα⁢αgα⁢α⁢ at the end of an increment,  and  1Rα=Bα⁢αGα⁢α⁢ at the beginning of the increment.  In these expressions, as in the following development, no summation is implied by a repeated index α. If the α-line is stretched uniformly by Δ⁢λα during the increment, we require that  rα=Δ⁢λα⁢Rα;  and, further, such uniform stretch of the shell must give constant strain so that since we assume  Δ⁢εα⁢α1=Δ⁢εα⁢α+η⁢λt⁢Δ⁢k~α⁢α,  we need  Δ⁢k~α⁢α=0  under such circumstances. In this motion  Δ⁢kα⁢α=1rα-1Δ⁢λt⁢Rα=1Rα(1Δ⁢λα-1Δ⁢λt)=1Rα⁢(1Δ⁢λα-1Δ⁢λt).  Defining  (9)Δ⁢k~α⁢α=Δ⁢kα⁢α+(1Δ⁢λt-1Δ⁢λα)⁢to⁢Bα⁢αGα⁢α  and assuming  (10)Δ⁢εα⁢α1=Δ⁢εα⁢α+η⁢λt⁢Δ⁢k~α⁢α  satisfies the requirement. [Equation 9](simathe-c-axishells.md#simathe-c-axishells-t-Theory-sma-topic2__simathe-c-axishells-eq9) may be simplified by substituting in the definition of Δ⁢kα⁢α in [Equation 7](simathe-c-axishells.md#simathe-c-axishells-t-Theory-sma-topic2__simathe-c-axishells-eq7) to give  Δ⁢k~α⁢α=bα⁢αgα⁢α-1Δ⁢λα⁢Bα⁢αGα⁢α,  and so  (11)Δ⁢k~α⁢α=1gα⁢α⁢(bα⁢α-Δ⁢λα⁢Bα⁢α).  The formulation is completed by the assumption that the virtual work equation can be written  (12)∫θ1,θ2{λto⁢∫hσα⁢β⁢δ⁢εα⁢β1⁢d⁢η+Tα⁢δ⁢γα}⁢(G11⁢G22)12⁢d⁢θ1⁢d⁢θ2=δ⁢WE,  where  σα⁢β  are the Kirchhoff stresses at a point;  (θ1,θ2)  in the shell, defined by plane stress theory using the summation of the strain increments in [Equation 10](simathe-c-axishells.md#simathe-c-axishells-t-Theory-sma-topic2__simathe-c-axishells-eq10) to define the strain at this point;  δ⁢εα⁢β1  are the variations of the strain increments in [Equation 10](simathe-c-axishells.md#simathe-c-axishells-t-Theory-sma-topic2__simathe-c-axishells-eq10);  Tα  are the transverse shear forces per unit area, defined by Tα=k⁢G⁢h⁢γα, where γα are the transverse shear strains from [Equation 8](simathe-c-axishells.md#simathe-c-axishells-t-Theory-sma-topic2__simathe-c-axishells-eq8), h is the original thickness of the shell, and k⁢G is the elastic transverse shear stiffness (reduced according to the suggestions of [Hughes et al. (1977)](simathe-c-references.md#simathe-c-references__simathe-c-ref-hughes-taylor) if the shell is too thin to avoid numerical problems); and  δ⁢WE  is the virtual external work rate.  This completes the statement of the formulation. |
