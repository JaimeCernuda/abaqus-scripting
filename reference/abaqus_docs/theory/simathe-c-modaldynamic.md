Structural damping in modal dynamic analysis Structural damping is a commonly used damping model that represents damping as complex stiffness. This representation causes no difficulty for frequency domain analysis such as steady-state dynamics for which the solution is already complex. However, in the time domain, the solution must remain real-valued. To allow users to apply their structural damping model in the time domain, a method has been developed to convert structural damping to an equivalent viscous damping. This technique was designed so that, in the frequency domain, the viscous damping applied is identical to structural damping if the projected damping matrix is diagonal.  We start with the equation of a single degree of freedom oscillator, m ⁢  x ¨ +  c ⁢  x ˙ +  k ⁢  (  1 +  i ⁢ γ ) ⁢ x = f , where m is the mass, c is the viscous damping factor, k is the stiffness, x is the response, f is the force, and   γ  is the structural damping factor. If we normalize by the mass and assume harmonic input, we obtain the relation (    ω 2 ⁢  (  1 +  i ⁢ γ ) -  Ω 2 +  i ⁢ 2 ⁢ ξ ⁢ ω ⁢ Ω ) ⁢  x 0 =  f 0 , where   ω  is the natural frequency,   Ω  is the excitation frequency,   x 0  is the coefficient of the harmonic response, and    f 0  is the coefficient of the harmonic input. The relation between the viscous damping factor c and the critical damping factor   ξ  is such that    c =  2 ⁢ ξ ⁢ ω ⁢ m .  Based on the two previous equations, if we want the viscous damping factor to have the same effect as the structural damping factor, we must have ξ =   ω ⁢ γ  2 ⁢ Ω .  If we further assume that    ω = Ω , the relation simplifies to ξ =  γ 2 .  Now we consider the finite element system of equations, M  N ⁢ M ⁢   x ¨ M +   C  N ⁢ M ⁢   x ˙ M +   (   K  N ⁢ M +  i ⁢  K s  N ⁢ M ) ⁢  x M =  f N , where    C  N ⁢ M  is the finite element viscous damping matrix and    K s  N ⁢ M  is the finite element structural damping matrix. If we project these equations onto the eigenmodes, the damping matrices become C ^ =    Φ N ⁢  C  N ⁢ M ⁢  Φ M          and             K ^ s =   Φ N ⁢  K s  N ⁢ M ⁢  Φ M , which could be fully populated. If we take the case that    C  N ⁢ M  and    K s  N ⁢ M  are diagonal, their diagonal elements can be written c ^ α =   2 ⁢  ξ α ⁢  ω α          and             k ^  s ⁢ α =   γ α ⁢  ω α 2 , where   α  is a particular mode. Maintaining the relation developed for the single degree of freedom system    ξ =  γ 2  for this diagonal case would require (   c ^  e ⁢ q ⁢ u ⁢ i ⁢ v ⁢ a ⁢ l ⁢ e ⁢ n ⁢ t ) α =    k ^  s ⁢ α  ω α .  For nondiagonal matrices the expression for equivalent viscous damping becomes C ^  e ⁢ q ⁢ u ⁢ i ⁢ v ⁢ a ⁢ l ⁢ e ⁢ n ⁢ t =  D ⁢   K ^ s ⁢ D , where D is a diagonal matrix whose entries are given by d α =  1   ω α .  Response of nodal and element variables The time integration is done in terms of the generalized coordinates, and the response of the physical variables is then immediately available by summation: u =   ∑ α   ϕ α ⁢  q α ,  ε =   ∑ α   ε α ⁢  q α ,  σ =   ∑ α   σ α ⁢  q α ,  R =   ∑ α   R α ⁢  q α , where    ϕ α  are the modes,    ε α  are the modal strain amplitudes,    σ α  are the modal stress amplitudes, and    R α  are the modal reaction force amplitudes corresponding to each eigenvector   α .  Initial conditions At the beginning of the step initial displacements and initial velocities must be converted to equivalent values of the generalized coordinates, which can be done exactly only if the number of eigenvectors equals the number of degrees of freedom. Since this is usually not the case, the initial values of the generalized coordinate displacement and velocity are calculated as q α =   1  m α ⁢  ϕ α M ⁢  M  M ⁢ N ⁢  x 0 N , where    m α  is the generalized mass for the eigenvector   α ,    ϕ α M  is the eigenvector,    M  M ⁢ N  is the mass matrix, and    x 0 N  are the initial displacements.  Similarly, for the initial velocities q ˙ α =   1  m α ⁢  ϕ α M ⁢  M  M ⁢ N ⁢   x ˙ 0 N .  For the case where the initial conditions are given by a previous modal dynamic analysis, the generalized displacement, velocity, and acceleration are simply taken from the previous analysis.
