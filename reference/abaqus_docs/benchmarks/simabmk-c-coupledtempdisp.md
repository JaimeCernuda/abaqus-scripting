# Coupled temperature-displacement analysis: one-dimensional gap conductance and radiation

| |  |  | | --- | --- | | Coupled temperature-displacement analysis: one-dimensional gap conductance and radiation | | |  | | | |  | | --- | | This example illustrates two elementary nonlinear cases of one-dimensional, fully coupled, heat transfer and stress analysis. The problems in this example are simple enough that exact solutions are obtained easily, thus providing verification of the numerical solutions obtained with Abaqus. A conductive rod of unit area is fixed at one end, A, and free at the other end, B. Between the free end and an adjacent fixed wall, C, there is a gap across which heat will be conducted or radiated.  In case 1 two forms of clearance-dependent heat transfer are considered: in the first, the conductivity for the gap drops linearly as the clearance increases; in the second, the gap radiation view factor drops linearly as the clearance increases. The fixed ends of the rod, A, and the wall, C, are both held at fixed temperatures, θA and θC. Initially the gap is open, so the distance between B and C is d0 (d0>0). The objective is to predict the steady-state displacement, uB, and temperature, θB, of the free end of the rod. We assume that the strains are small and that the behavior of the rod is linear elastic, with constant modulus and thermal expansion coefficient. In this case the gap never closes, so the rod is always stress-free.  In case 2 it is assumed that the conductivity across the closed gap increases linearly as the pressure transmitted through the gap, p, increases. The fixed end of the rod, A, and the wall, C (which is also fixed in position), are both held at fixed temperatures, θA and θC. Since in this case the gap never opens, the axial stress in the rod will be nonzero. We solve for the pressure across the gap, p, and the temperature, θB, of the end of the rod, assuming that the strains are small and the behavior of the rod is linear elastic with constant modulus and thermal expansion coefficient.  In Abaqus/Standard the bar is modeled with either two- or three-dimensional elements; the contact between the end of the bar and the wall is modeled in one of three ways: as a gap element (GAPUNIT) or as an element-based rigid surface made of T2D2T, S4RT, S4T, or S8RT elements. In Abaqus/Explicit the bar is modeled with either two- or three-dimensional elements; the wall is modeled one of two ways: either as an analytical rigid surface or as an element-based rigid surface. Surface-based contact is employed between the bar and the wall; both kinematic and penalty mechanical contact are considered.  Solution Mechanical equilibrium along the rod requires that  d⁢σd⁢S=0,  where S is the distance along the rod measured from the fixed end, A. Integrating along the rod, the stress is  σ⁢(S)=-p,  where p is the pressure transmitted by contact between the end of the rod, B, and the adjacent fixed point, C.  Thermal equilibrium requires that the heat flux along the rod, q, has no gradient:  d⁢qd⁢S=0.  Integrating this along the rod and imposing the boundary condition that the flux at B is the same as the flux transmitted from B to C through the gap, qg, then gives the thermal equilibrium equation  q⁢(S)=qg.  Since we assume that the strains are small, the strain at any point in the rod is  ε⁢(S)=d⁢ud⁢S,  and the displacement is  u⁢(S)=∫ε⁢(S)⁢d⁢S.  The rod is assumed to be made of a linear elastic material, so the stress constitutive equation is  σ=E⁢(ε-α⁢θ),  where the modulus, E, and the thermal expansion coefficient, α, are constants (they are not temperature dependent).  Heat conduction in the rod is assumed to be governed by Fourier's law, which states that the heat flux is determined by  q=-kr⁢d⁢θd⁢S,  where kr is the thermal conductivity of the rod and is also assumed to be constant. Combining thermal equilibrium with the Fourier law in the rod shows that d⁢θ/d⁢S is constant in the rod, so the temperature, θ, varies linearly along the rod:  θ=θA+SL⁢(θB-θA),  where L is the length of the rod.  The heat flux in the gap, qg, between the end of the rod, B, and the fixed point C is assumed to be proportional to the difference in temperature between B and C:  qg=kg⁢(θB-θC),    kg≥0.  Case 1 First we assume that the gap is open and that the gap thermal conductivity, kg, increases linearly as the gap reduces, so  kg=max⁡(ko+ku′⁢uB,0),  where k0 and ku′ are nonnegative constants and uB is the displacement of point B. Gap radiation is neglected in these calculations.  The thermal boundary conditions are that the temperatures at A and C, θA and θC, are held constant. The mechanical boundary conditions are that points A and C are fixed. Since in this case the end of the rod never touches C, force equilibrium requires that  σ=-p=0.  The equations given above define the problem. Their solution is readily developed as follows. Combining integrated force equilibrium with the linear elastic constitutive equation and the displacement relationship, u⁢(S), gives  uB=α⁢L2⁢(θA+θB).  Thermal equilibrium combined with Fourier's law and the gap heat flux equation then gives  krL⁢(θA-θB)=kg⁢(θB-θC).  With the assumed form of the gap thermal conductivity, kg, and assuming kg>​ 0, this is  krL⁢(θA-θB)=(k0+ku′⁢uB)⁢(θB-θC),    kg>0.  Substituting for uB then gives a quadratic equation for θB:  θB2+2⁢(1ku′⁢α⁢L⁢(krL+k0)+12⁢(θA-θC))-θA⁢θC-2ku′⁢α⁢L⁢(k0⁢θC+krL⁢θA)=0,    kg>0.  The roots of this quadratic equation provide two solutions for θB. The solutions for uB are available once θB is determined. Only one of the two solutions gives a value of uB for which kg>​ 0; hence, this is the only physically acceptable solution.  As a numerical example the parameters are chosen in consistent units as kr=k0=​ 1.0; α=​10−5, L=​ 1.0; ku′=​ 100; θA=​ 400°; and θC=​ 200°.  These values give θB=​ 285.4° or −4485°, so uB=​3.427 × 10−3 or −2.043 × 10−2. The second solution must be rejected as it gives kg<​ 0. The first solution is valid so long as d0>uB.  Next we assume that the gap is open and that the gap radiation view factor, Fg, increases linearly as the gap reduces; so  Fg=max⁡(Fo+Fu′⁢uB,0),  where F0 and Fu′ are nonnegative constants and uB is the displacement of point B. In this case gap conduction is neglected.  The thermal and mechanical boundary conditions are the same as the gap conduction problem considered above. Force equilibrium requires that  σ=-p=0.  Following a procedure similar to that used in the gap conduction problem and combining integrated force equilibrium with the linear elastic constitutive equation and the displacement relationship, u⁢(S), gives  uB=α⁢L2⁢(θA+θB).  Combining thermal equilibrium with Fourier's law and the gap heat flux equation then gives  krL⁢(θA-θB)=qg.  With the assumed form of the gap radiation, qg, and assuming Fg>​ 0, this is  krL⁢(θA-θB)=σ⁢ϵ⁢(F0+Fu′⁢uB)⁢(θB4-θC4),    Fg>0.  Substituting for uB then gives the following equation for θB:  θB5+(θA+2⁢F0Fu′⁢α⁢L)⁢θB4-(θC4-2⁢KrFu′⁢α⁢σ⁢ϵ⁢L2)⁢θB⁢​-(θA⁢θC4+2⁢KrFu′⁢α⁢σ⁢ϵ⁢L2⁢θA+2⁢F0Fu′⁢α⁢L⁢θC4)=0⁢​  θB is obtained by solving the above equation numerically. The solutions for uB are available once θB is determined.  As a numerical example the parameters are chosen in consistent units as F0=​ 1.0; α=​10−5; L=​ 1.0; Fu′=​ 50; σ=​ 1.E−8; ϵ=​1.0; θA=​ 400°; θC=​ 200°; and absolute zero = –460.  These values give θB=​ 222.4°, so uB=​ 3.112 × 10−3. This solution is valid so long as d0>uB. All other solutions must be rejected since they give Fg<0 or d0<uB.  Case 2 In this case the rod is always in contact with C. Combining the integrated equilibrium equation with the mechanical constitutive model gives  p=-E⁢(ε-α⁢θ).  Combining this with the temperature solution, θ, gives  p=-E⁢(ε-α⁢(θA+SL⁢(θB-θA))).  Integrating this along the rod using the displacement relationship, u⁢(S), gives the pressure as a function of the temperature at points A and B,  p=E⁢α2⁢(θA+θB)  since uB=0.  In this case the conductivity of the closed gap is proportional to the contact pressure:  kg=ka+kp′⁢p,  where ka and kp′ are nonnegative constants. Since kr⁢(θA-θB)/L=kg⁢(θB-θC), with this behavior for kg we have  krL⁢(θA-θB)=(ka+kp′⁢p)⁢(θB-θC),    p>0.  Combining this with the equation for the pressure provides a quadratic equation for θB:  θB2+2⁢(1kp′⁢E⁢α⁢(krL+ka)+12⁢(θA-θC))⁢θB-θA⁢θC-2kp′⁢E⁢α⁢(ka⁢θC+krL⁢θA)=0,    p>0.  The roots of this equation provide two solutions for θB, and the corresponding values of p are then defined by p=E⁢α⁢(θA+θB)/2. Only one solution gives a positive value for p; the other must be rejected because it is inconsistent with the assumption that the gap is closed.  As a numerical example the parameters are chosen in consistent units as kr=​ 10; ka=​ 2; kp′=​ 0.2; α=​ 10−5; E=​ 105; L=​ 1.0; θA=​ 200°; and θC=​ 100°.  These values give θB=​ 122.6° or −342.6°, so p=​ 161.3 or −71.3. The second solution must be rejected as it gives p<0.  Results and discussion In both cases Abaqus/Standard uses a full Newton method and obtains the solution in one or two increments requiring two or three iterations per increment. The values for uB and θB in case 1 and p and θB in case 2 agree with the exact solutions obtained above.  The results obtained with Abaqus/Explicit also agree with the analytical solutions.
